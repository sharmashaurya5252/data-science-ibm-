Chapter 1 · The scale

₹7.52 Cr

That is what 1,000 Flipkart orders brought in over twelve months, leaving ₹1.50 Cr in profit. The average basket was about ₹75,000, and the typical (median) one ₹57,000: a few very large orders pull the average up.

Chapter 2 · The rhythm

## No festive spike, just a steady drumbeat

Full months land between about ₹50 L and ₹76 L, with July the high point at ₹76.4 L and June the low at ₹49.9 L. The first and last months look small only because the data starts on 12 Feb 2024 and stops on 9 Feb 2025.

Chapter 3 · The money

## Big-ticket orders carry the business

Items priced ₹40–50k are 18% of orders but a third of revenue (33.6%). Items under ₹10k are 20% of orders and just 4.2% of revenue. If you have one lever, it is the premium end.

Chapter 4 · The mix

## Electronics leads, but nobody is far behind

Electronics earns 23.0% of revenue; Home & Kitchen, the smallest, still earns 17.7%. Regions are similarly balanced: North leads with 27% and East trails at 22%, a gap that is not statistically significant (p = 0.80).

Chapter 5 · The surprise

## Discounts did not buy sales or smiles

Discounts ranged from 5% to 30%. Orders at 25–30% off were no larger than those at 5–10%, and ratings did not improve either (correlation with rating +0.01, p = 0.65). Deeper discounts cost margin without a measurable return in this data.

Chapter 6 · The honest limits

## What this data cannot tell us

Profit is exactly 20% of sales on every order, so margin cannot be compared across products or discounts. Prices do not vary by category (books average ₹25k, like laptops), and ratings are spread evenly from 1 to 5. These patterns suggest a simulated dataset, so treat the findings as a demonstration of method rather than a market truth.

**Recommendations:** (1) lean into premium-priced items; (2) test discount depth in a controlled experiment rather than assuming it works; (3) capture real cost data so true profitability can be analysed.
